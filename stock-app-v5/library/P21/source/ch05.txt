    We have specified five kinds of change that con-
stitute a dynamic condition.        All of them are in prog-
ress in a modern society, and all are acting on its
structural   form.     As population     increases, the new
laborers apportion        themselves in an irregular way
among the different groups and sub-groups into which
the producing       society is divided, and some of the
sub-groups     increase more rapidly than others.         As
capital increases, there is the same irregularity      in the
apportionment      of it; for some of the sub-groups get a
disproportionate      share of the new productive fund.
A theory of economic dynamics should tell by what
principle these apportionments       are governed.
    Mechanical     inventions, in particular, are obvious
disturbers of the group relations.          Much labor sav-
ing in one part of the system is, so far as it goes,
the cause of a natural drifting of labor to other parts.
New kinds of goods call for new industrial groups
to make them, and these are created by taking men
and capital from old ones. Thus, every one of the
general changes that we have specified, as keeping
society in a dynamic state, declares its presence by
acting on the social structure.        Mere industry is the Whystatic
